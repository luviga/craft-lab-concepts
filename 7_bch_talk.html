From buildings to bulk

Distribution grid experiments for electrification planning under uncertainty

An open laboratory, from buildings up to a regional plan. It tests whether the plan changes when it can see the feeders.

Electricity demand is growing again, at a speed nobody knows

Two kinds of growth

  • Large loads, such as data centres, connect at transmission
  • Homes, businesses and vehicles connect on feeders, street by street
  • Both draw on the same generation and transmission

Forecasts span a wide range

  • US data centres used 4.4% of electricity in 2023. Forecasts for 2028 run from 6.7% to 12%.
  • California used 281 TWh in 2023. The forecast for 2040 is 376 TWh.
  • California's grid upgrade bill for 2040 runs from $17 to $38 billion, depending on how cars charge.

The planning problem. A plan has to hold across that range.

TWh is a terawatt-hour, a billion kilowatt-hours.

Distributed resources can meet part of it, if plans can price them by place

New wires

Bigger transformers, new lines, new feeders. Paid once.

Flexibility from distributed resources

Managed car charging, batteries, solar, flexible heating. They shift or cut the peak. Paid every year.

  • Peak cut on a feeder can free capacity upstream
  • Regional plans are too coarse to capture local reinforcement and flexibility (Jacquier et al., 2025)
  • Feeders differ, so the value of flexibility differs by place
  • Investment in local grid equipment per customer doubled in real terms, 2005 to 2024

Local grid equipment is the poles, wires and transformers between a substation and its customers. Accountants call it distribution plant.

A feeder is one circuit from a substation to its customers.

Distributed resources are customer devices that can change when power is used.

Annex A2. Supports Resources and Approach.

One feeder shows how the two answers compare in dollars

1 New wires

A 12 MW feeder costs $3.5 million, paid once

2 One year of delay

Building one year later is worth about $167,000, at a 5% discount rate

3 Flexibility

Managed charging costs $112 per car a year. $167,000 pays for about 1,500 cars.

  • If 1,500 managed cars are enough, waiting is cheaper
  • If the feeder needs more cars, new wires are cheaper
  • The experiment measures that number by simulating each feeder
  • The grid is NREL's synthetic feeders (SMART-DS). The load is NREL's building profiles (EULP).
  • This is a one-year example. The full comparison runs over 20 years.

Delay means building the feeder one year later, because flexibility keeps the peak under the rating for that year.

A discount rate is the yearly value of money. A cost paid later counts for less than one paid today.

California, by 2040

About $25 billion of upgrades. Managed charging could avoid $5 to $18 billion, before program costs (Cal Advocates, 2025).

Annex A7. Supports Regional plan.

No result yet, but studies and first steps show what to expect

What we expect to see

  • Plans change more where feeders are tight
  • Cost may move more than built capacity, as in France
  • A grid-blind plan may put solar where feeders cannot host it
  • It may miss flexibility that would delay upgrades
  • These are expectations. The runs will test them.

What we have learned so far

  1. Cost data are scattered across utilities, years and units
  2. Prices move fast. One transformer cost 2.8 times more in 2025 than in 2018, after inflation.
  3. Synthetic feeders need weighting to observed demand
  4. Feeder simulation puts wires and flexibility in one unit, dollars per MW of peak relief
