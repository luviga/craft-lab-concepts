From buildings to bulk: the chapter concepts

Each chapter's idea, before any procedure. Every number on this page is invented to show a mechanism. None is a result.

All three chapters use exploratory modelling (Bankes, 1993). Chapter 1 is the trunk.

Chapters 1 and 2 are simulation. Chapter 3 adds the one optimization.

Chapter 1

When is flexibility cheaper than reinforcing a feeder, and what decides it?

A feeder absorbs new load up to a point, the onset load. Past it, the utility either reinforces equipment or uses flexibility to keep the peak down. Chapter 1 measures which is cheaper across many feeders, and tests whether climate and adoption decide it more than feeder layout.

Method family
Cost-benefit analysis of planning alternatives. Each alternative is sized by the violations it resolves and compared by net present value (Quirós-Tortós and Ochoa, 2021).
Why it could go either way
Flexibility suits short peaks just past the onset load. Reinforcement suits long or large overloads, and feeders where upgrades are cheap. Peak length and overload size follow climate and adoption. Upgrade cost follows feeder layout.
What varies, and what is fixed
Feeders vary in layout and climate-demand zone. Adoption level rises. Unit costs and the rules for each option stay fixed.
What comes in, and what goes out
Building profiles come in, coupled to each feeder by the methods paper. Feeders and cost curves go to Chapter 2. The feeder surrogate goes to Chapter 3: onset load, cost adder and peak relief.
What would show it wrong
Feeder archetype explains more of the cost gap than zone and adoption level do. Then layout decides the ranking. Chapter 3's fixed archetype shares would then carry more weight.

Chapter 2

Which conditions stress feeders, and which pathway holds up across futures?

Nobody knows how adoption will unfold. Chapter 2 runs many futures without assigning them probabilities. It finds which conditions cause stress, and which pathway, reinforcement or flexibility, carries the least regret.

Why it belongs
Chapter 2 tests the answer of Chapter 1. It asks whether the ranking of reinforcement and flexibility holds across futures with no assigned probabilities. It samples many uncertain factors together, which Chapters 1 and 3 set at fixed levels. PRIM finds, from the runs, which conditions and lever ranges cause stress. It also tells distribution planners which pathway has the least regret.
Method family
The analytic steps of robust decision making (Lempert et al., 2003). The steps are XLRM framing, uniform sampling within bounds, and scenario discovery with PRIM (Friedman and Fisher, 1999; Bryant and Lempert, 2010). The last step is regret over pathways (Savage, 1951). Stakeholder deliberation is outside the thesis.
Regret
Regret is how much more a pathway costs in one future than the best pathway in that future.
Rs,i = Cs,i − mins′ Cs′,i
The criterion that ranks pathways by regret is chosen before the runs.
Why it could go either way
Flexibility may hold in most futures and fail in a few, such as fast unmanaged heating. Reinforcement costs more up front and has no such failure. The winner depends on how many futures fail, and how badly.
What varies, and what is fixed
Adoption, lever settings and unit costs vary within bounds, with no probabilities attached. The feeders come from Chapter 1 and stay fixed.
What comes in, and what goes out
Feeders and cost curves come in from Chapter 1. Stress regions go out as readable rules, with the least-regret pathway per feeder type. Nothing goes to Chapter 3.
What would show it wrong
Stress regions fail to hold on a fresh sample. If one pathway has the least regret everywhere, Chapter 1's ranking holds without the ensemble.

Chapter 3

What does seeing feeders change in a national plan?

National plans often treat distribution as free, or as one flat cost. Chapter 3 gives the national model each node's feeder costs and checks whether the plan changes. It changes only if those costs shift the balance between reinforcing, flexing and supplying from upstream.

Method family
A one-way soft link carries feeder results to the national model (Chang et al., 2023). The substitution triangle says why the plan could change: it shows where the plan spends to meet new peak. With and without runs measure the change (Jacquier et al., 2025), extended to three settings. One optimization model is used in exploratory mode (Bankes, 1993). A fractional factorial and a materiality rule say how far the result holds (NIST/SEMATECH).
Why it could go either way
Feeder costs may be small next to generation and fuel. They may also tip the balance at nodes where the onset load binds early. Deane et al. (2012) and Jacquier et al. (2025) found that missing grid detail changed flexibility and curtailment.
What varies, and what is fixed
The setting varies: blind, flat-adder or feeder-informed. Standard national inputs vary between low and high values. The model and the archetype shares per node stay fixed.
What comes in, and what goes out
The feeder surrogate comes in from Chapter 1, per archetype, zone and adoption level. Nothing comes in from Chapter 2. The value of visibility and the value of detail go out, as build change and system cost difference.
What would show it wrong
Feeder detail moves total system cost less than standard national inputs, such as fuel cost and demand. Then a flat adder suffices for national planning.

Terms used on this page

The chapter that uses each term is in brackets.

Onset load
Feeder-head load at which the first thermal or voltage violation appears. (1, 3)
Reinforcement
New or larger equipment that removes violations. (1, 2)
Flexibility
DER and demand management that lower the peak, with no upgrades. (1, 2)
Peak relief
The MW of peak that full flexibility removes. Past it, flexibility alone runs out. (1, 3)
Cost gap
Net present cost of reinforcement minus that of flexibility, for one feeder. Positive means flexibility is cheaper. (1)
Future
One sampled set of uncertain inputs, with no probability attached. (2)
Pathway
One intervention plan, applied in every future. (2)
Hybrid
A pathway that uses flexibility first and reinforces for the rest. (2)
Regret
How much more a pathway costs in one future than the best pathway in that future. (2)
Stress region
A box of input ranges where stressed futures concentrate, found by PRIM. (2)
Setting
How the national model sees distribution: blind, flat-adder or feeder-informed. (3)
Substitution triangle
Where a node's plan spends to meet new peak: link reinforcement, flexibility behind the link, or upstream supply. Reinforcement and upstream supply often go together. Flexibility substitutes for both. (3)
Materiality
Feeder detail matters if its cost effect is at least that of two of the four standard inputs. Preliminary. (3)
