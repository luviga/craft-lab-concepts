Demand grows again. Not in the same shape, the same place, or at a known speed.

Heat pumps and electric vehicles (EVs) add load street by street. Data centers arrive in blocks of hundreds of megawatts (MW). National plans usually see one cost per region. This page shows why that one number can hide the answer.

Feeder
The local circuit from one substation transformer to its customers.
Feeder limit
The load at which a feeder first exceeds an equipment rating or a voltage limit.
Flexibility
Managed charging, heat-pump pre-heating, rooftop solar, and batteries, used to cut a feeder peak.
Large load
One connection of tens to hundreds of MW, such as a data center.
National plan
The generation, storage, and transmission that a national capacity expansion model proposes for each region.

Nobody knows the speed

Street load grows with each heat pump and each car that people buy. The speed depends on prices, policy, and habits.

Large loads arrive in steps. A request can connect in two years, in ten, or never.

In Alberta, 29 proposed data center projects asked for more than 16 gigawatts (GW) of grid connections in 2025. The system operator set an interim limit of 1,200 MW on new large loads until 2028.

In the United States, data centers used 4.4% of electricity in 2023. Projections for 2028 range from 6.7% to 12%.

Each line is one possible future for one region: street load plus any large loads that connect. The thick line is the latest draw. The bounds are illustrative. No future carries a probability.

Both kinds of load draw on the same upstream capacity

Street load and large loads compete for the same generation and transmission. A megawatt that flexibility removes on a feeder is a megawatt free upstream.

A plan that cannot see feeders cannot count that megawatt. It also cannot see the feeders that need new wires anyway.

Distribution costs rise too. Since 2014, growth in U.S. investor-owned utility distribution costs accounts for over 30% of the national-average rate increase.

The question the thesis tests

When a national plan can see how individual feeders respond to electrification, does it propose a different build? Which feeder and demand conditions cause the difference?

The thesis solves the same national model three times, with the same inputs, and compares the plans.

Blind

The plan sees no distribution cost. Every feeder is invisible.

Flat

The plan charges one cost per MW in every region. Every feeder looks like the average feeder.

Feeder-informed

Each region gets three values from feeder simulations: the load at which problems start, the cost of each further MW, and the peak that flexibility removes.
